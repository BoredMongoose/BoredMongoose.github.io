Environment & health · Delhi

Who's really choking Delhi: Diwali crackers or Punjab's stubble fires?

The answer

Both, but not equally. Stubble smoke drives about 60% of Delhi's PM2.5 in the first ten days of November. Diwali night is one of the five worst nights of every year, but it's short: over a season it adds about 2% as much pollution as the stubble-burning period. Over the whole winter, about 84% is Delhi's own pollution, trapped by the weather.

Data
Delhi air quality 2015–2020 (CPCB), 600,000+ NASA satellite fire detections, hourly weather
Period
Five winters, 2015–2019
Tools
Python: pandas, statsmodels, scikit-learn
Code
github.com/BoredMongoose/delhi-smog ↗

Results

How I did it

  1. Started with the obvious approach: regress daily pollution on the number of fires. It gave a big, "significant" effect.
  2. Tested it with a placebo: I fed the model the wrong year's fires. The fake fires "explained" pollution just as well, which meant the model was picking up the season, not the smoke. So I dropped that approach.
  3. Switched to weather normalisation: learn how weather drives Delhi's pollution in months with almost no fires (mid-December to March), predict October–November from the weather alone, and treat the gap as autumn's extra pollution.
  4. Checked it: the gap is close to zero before burning starts and after it ends, it's larger when the wind comes from Punjab, and five different model versions all put it at 40–46% of PM2.5 from mid-October to the end of November.

What could make this wrong

  • The "extra" pollution is autumn-only pollution. Its timing and wind pattern point to the fires, but other autumn sources are mixed in.
  • The measured air-quality data ends in mid-2020, so it can't show whether Delhi's air improved as fires fell after 2021.
  • Five winters is a small sample.
